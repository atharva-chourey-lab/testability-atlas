# The Testability Atlas: Which Questions in Indian Historical Statistics Can Surviving Data Decide?

Author: Atharva Chourey
Affiliation: Atharva Chourey Lab (independent research lab)

*Manuscript in preparation — not yet peer-reviewed.*

## Abstract

Of the 23 questions graded in this Atlas, 4 are testable, 11 are underpowered, and 8 are untestable on clean Indian official data. The Atlas does not test theories. It grades rulers: for each pairing of an Indian official series and a question the literature treats as meaningful, it asks whether the series' clean span — its longest span on a single base, method, or design, with no silent splicing — could detect an effect of the literature-sized magnitude at conventional power. The grid covers 10 series and 8 tests. Only beta convergence across states, the Kitchin cycle in bank credit, the Kitchin cycle in merchandise trade values, and the political budget cycle in state finances clear the testable threshold. Every rebased index fails or is capped in the business-cycle cells; gross domestic product, the flagship series, is the weakest cycle ruler in the grid. Nineteen of the 23 questions cannot be answered at literature-sized effects on clean data. That count, computed under a pre-registered rubric, machinery, and anchors, is the result. A verdict of untestable is not evidence against a theory, and a verdict of testable is not evidence for it.

*Keywords:* India; official statistics; statistical power; minimum detectable effect; pre-registration; business cycles; data quality

## 1. Introduction: Argument Without Decidability

Many arguments in Indian macroeconomics and economic history share a structure that is rarely examined directly. Two sides cite estimates from the same official series, disagree about what they show, and continue — without first establishing whether the series, as it survives, could settle a disagreement of that size. An estimate can be precisely reported and still be uninformative if the span is too short, the base has changed too often, the design has been restarted, or the noise in the ruler is large relative to the effect the literature treats as meaningful. Before testing comes a prior question: is the question testable at all, on the data that actually exist?

This paper reports a systematic attempt to ask that prior question for India's headline official statistics. We call the exercise the Testability Atlas. Its unit of analysis is a pairing: a series, in the form in which it can be used without splicing across breaks, together with a specific test drawn from the literature that uses it. For each pairing we compute, or apply a registered rule to determine, the minimum detectable effect (MDES) — the smallest effect a test at $\alpha = 0.05$ and power $0.80$ could reliably detect — and compare it with an anchor, the effect size the relevant literature treats as meaningful, fixed in advance. The comparison yields one of three verdicts: testable, underpowered, or untestable.

The language discipline matters as much as the arithmetic, and was registered before the build. The Atlas grades the ruler, not the theory. A verdict is a statement about Indian official data — in the registration's wording, that "on this series' clean span, a test could / could barely / could not detect an effect of the size the literature treats as meaningful." Untestable is not evidence against a theory; testable is not evidence for it. An untestable verdict on the Juglar cycle in gross domestic product (GDP) does not refute Juglar cycles in India; it says the clean quarterly GDP span is too short for that band, so estimates in that band cannot carry the weight of a decisive test. A testable verdict on the political budget cycle says only that the state-finance panel can detect an effect of the anchored size; it says nothing about whether such an effect exists.

Grading should precede testing for three reasons. First, it redirects effort: if a cell is untestable by a span rule, no estimator within that span can rescue it — the responses are to recover a longer ruler, change the design so the same records answer a gradeable question, or suspend the argument. Second, it exposes ruler disagreement: the two monsoon rulers disagree not only about rainfall levels but about testability itself in two bands. Third, it is auditable: each verdict is tied to a clean span, an MDES and an anchor, disputable at the level of a specific input.

The headline result is stark: 19 of the 23 questions cannot be answered at literature-sized effects on clean data. Testability tracks ruler stability, not importance — the clearest passes are levels (bank credit, trade values) with no base year; GDP is the worst cycle ruler; and the same records can flip verdicts with design, as the political budget cycle shows.

This exercise has published precedents, and we claim no novelty for its method or for undecidability arguments in general. Field-scale power grading in economics was established by Ioannidis, Stanley and Doucouliagos (2017), who surveyed 159 empirical economics literatures and graded the statistical power of published estimates rather than of questions. Qualitative arguments that Indian historical data cannot decide old questions are also long-standing: Tomlinson (1993; 2nd ed. 2013) argued that colonial data are misleading, deceptive or partial and that quantitative attempts at definitive answers to questions including national-income fluctuations, famine subsistence, deindustrialisation, landholdings and poverty have convinced few sceptics. The contribution claimed here is narrower, in exactly that narrowed form: the first question-level testability/power grading scoped specifically to Indian historical statistics, applying an established method to 23 standard questions. It is not the first power audit in economics, and it is not the first claim that Indian historical data cannot decide old questions.

Section 2 describes the methods as pre-registered; Section 3 presents the full table and the patterns in what fails; Section 4 discusses implications, what could move cells, and the grading's limitations. The Atlas reuses some pilot numbers (monsoon and IIP persistence, the IMD–IITM comparison, pilot band verdicts); this paper imports no results from other projects in the programme, and reports relationships to other builds only as the Atlas files state them.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Question selection: the grid

The grid was fixed in the pre-registration before any new data were pulled for this build: 10 series × 8 tests = 80 pairs. The series are: (S1) monsoon rainfall (June–September, all-India), on two rulers — the India Meteorological Department (IMD) operational series and the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology (IITM) homogeneous series — graded jointly with both reported; (S2) GDP and national accounts, including state GSDP; (S3) the Index of Industrial Production (IIP); (S4) the Consumer Price Index (CPI) Combined; (S5) the Wholesale Price Index (WPI); (S6) bank credit (scheduled commercial bank non-food credit outstanding); (S7) merchandise trade (exports, values); (S8) state finances; (S9) unemployment from the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS); and (S10) groundwater stage of extraction from the Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) assessments. The tests are: (T1) Kitchin cycle, 3–5 years; (T2) Juglar cycle, 7–11 years; (T3) monsoon periodicities in three sub-bands (ENSO 2–7 years, solar 9.5–12.5 years, long 17–21 years); (T4) Phillips curve slope; (T5) Okun coefficient; (T6) political budget cycle, in Khemani's design; (T7) beta convergence, with sigma convergence as a sub-grade; and (T8) Taylor rule / natural rate ($r^*$) estimation of the Laubach–Williams class.

Applicability was fixed in advance, so pairings the literature does not make are not manufactured into untestability: S1 is graded only at T3, and T3 only at S1; Phillips where the inflation ruler lives (S4, S5), GDP supplying the partner gap; Okun at S9; the political budget cycle only at S8; convergence only at S2; $r^*$ only at S4. S10 is not applicable on any column — its waves are irregular cross-sections, not a fixed-frequency series — and its audit row exists, in the registration's words, because an honest grid must show a load-bearing series none of the registered theories can use.

Counting follows the build's own accounting. There are 21 graded series-by-test pairs; because T3 is graded once per band, they produce 23 graded rows — the 23 questions of the headline counts. The machine-readable grid has 82 rows (the 80 pairs, with the monsoon pair expanded into three band rows): 23 graded and 59 not applicable, each NA with its registered one-line reason. The sigma sub-grade is reported within the T7 cell; it is not an additional graded question and is not counted in the 23.

### 2.2 The clean span and the ruler rules

Six registered rules (R1–R6) govern what counts as a ruler. R1, no silent splicing, is the foundation: a clean span is the longest span on a single base, method or design, and all sample sizes, MDES values and verdicts use it only; spliced spans may be mentioned, labelled "spliced", and are never graded. Hence GDP's long published span yields a clean quarterly span of only 58 quarters on the 2011-12 base. R2 requires each clean span's end to be stated with its cause (base change, method change, design revamp, or data end). R3: cross-ruler gaps are measured only over identical periods; unmeasurable gaps are recorded as "documented-only" or "pending", never estimated. R4: only revision behaviour attested in an official release, calendar or cited study enters the audit; anything requiring a vintages dataset is marked pending the vintages build ("pending D"). R5: like-for-like pairing — a regression cell is graded on the pairing matching the literature design's population and concept, so urban-only current-weekly-status unemployment is not graded against national output for Okun. R6: no observation dropping — COVID-era observations inside a clean span stay in, entering through the registered full-span noise and persistence inputs; no outlier rule was registered, so none was applied. Each series was audited on a fixed schema (ruler, frequency, published and clean spans, breaks, second ruler, cross-ruler gap as measured / documented-only / pending, revision behaviour, sample sizes, and inputs as real or assumed).

### 2.3 MDES machinery

Common settings: $\alpha = 0.05$, target power $0.80$, and for regressions the multiplier $(z_{0.975} + z_{0.80}) = 1.960 + 0.842 = 2.802$.

*Spectral family (T1–T3).* The test is the pilot's registered rule plus a registered injection step. Series are demeaned and detrended; the statistic is the maximum within-band power of a Hann-tapered periodogram on an eight-times zero-padded grid, against the 95th percentile over 2,000 AR(1) surrogates (seed 42). A sinusoid at the band's centre period is injected into same-length surrogates at amplitudes $0.10$–$1.20$ sd, 200 trials per amplitude (seed 43, random phase); the MDES is the smallest amplitude with power $\geq 0.80$, interpolated. Span rules override the simulation: under twice the longest period is untestable (fewer than two cycles cannot identify a cycle against trend); under four times, capped at underpowered. Anchors: 10 per cent of variance ($\approx 0.47$ sd) for monsoon bands — Kane's (1990) periodicities were dominant features, so a weaker cycle is not the literature's claim — and 15 per cent ($\approx 0.59$ sd) for business-cycle bands, the conservative end of the Burns–Mitchell share as represented in the registration.

*Regression family (T4–T7).* $\mathrm{MDES}(\beta) = 2.802 \times \sigma_\varepsilon / (\mathrm{sd}(x) \times \sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}})$, designs and inputs fixed in the registration; panels deflate $n$ by a registered design effect. Minimum-$n$ rules apply regardless of the formula: time-series cells with $n < 20$ are untestable (formula reported "for the record"); cross-sections require $\geq 20$ units; panels $\geq 200$ unit-years and $\geq 15$ clusters. Anchors: Phillips $0.25$ pp/pp (middle of published Indian estimates as surveyed in the registration); Okun $0.30$ (advanced-economy rule of thumb); political budget cycle $0.25$ pp of GSDP on capital outlay (primary) and $0.50$ pp on the deficit, set below developing-country averages because Khemani (2004) found Indian effects deliberately small; beta convergence $1.5$ %/yr (Cashin & Sahay 1996), sigma against a drift of $0.001$–$0.003$/yr.

*Structural family (T8).* Grading is by span rule, reflecting the literature's practice of using at least 40 years of quarterly data on consistent rulers: a clean joint span below 100 quarters is untestable, 100–159 underpowered, $\geq 160$ testable. Published Reserve Bank of India (RBI) $r^*$ estimates of 1.4–1.9 per cent are recorded as existing estimates; the verdict is about data-only testability under R1, since they rest on spliced series plus model structure.

Inputs divide into real and assumed, preserved in every output. Persistence for both monsoon rulers and the IIP is real (pilot data). Persistence for GDP, CPI, WPI, credit and trade, and all regression noise and dispersion inputs, are registered assumptions anchored to published magnitudes, not revisable after MDES output was seen.

### 2.4 The grading rubric

As defined in the pre-registration: for spectral cells, testable iff MDES $\leq$ anchor and span $\geq 4 \times$ longest period; underpowered iff MDES $\leq 2 \times$ anchor and span $\geq 2 \times$ longest period and not testable; untestable iff MDES $> 2 \times$ anchor or span $< 2 \times$ longest period. For regression cells, testable iff MDES $\leq$ anchor; underpowered iff anchor $<$ MDES $\leq 2 \times$ anchor; untestable iff MDES $> 2 \times$ anchor or a minimum-$n$ or pairing rule decides. For the structural cell, the §2.3 span thresholds define the classes. Monsoon rows are graded jointly, as the worse of the two rulers; the source figures split the rulers so their disagreement stays visible.

### 2.5 Pre-registration note

The Atlas was pre-registered on 2026-11-17; at registration no new data had been pulled for this build and the only numbers in hand were the pilot's. The grid, machinery, anchors, thresholds, designs and inputs above are as registered, and the build records the registration's commit identifier (`4167fc2`). One erratum is recorded in the build rather than corrected silently: the registration's summary gave the NA count as 57; the correct count is 59 of the 82 grid rows — the grid implemented is exactly the registered grid. Also recorded: injected sinusoids use random phase (conservative; the registration did not fix phase), and the code's periodogram was checked against a standard implementation on the pilot IMD series (band maxima within 1 per cent). No cell was added, dropped or re-graded after computation.

### 3.2 Patterns in what fails

*Testability tracks ruler stability, not importance.* Bank credit and trade values pass the Kitchin test because they are levels — rupees and dollars, no base year, no basket — with 23 and 26 clean years respectively in the build's rounding. Every rebased index fails or is capped. Each rebasing restarts the clean clock, and a series rebased roughly once a decade can never, on clean data, accumulate the four full Juglar cycles a full grade requires.

*GDP is the worst cycle ruler in the grid.* A Kitchin cycle in quarterly GDP growth would have to explain about 35 per cent of variance (MDES 1.04 sd) to be detectable at 80 per cent power on the 58 clean quarters of the 2011-12 base, against a 15 per cent anchor; the Juglar cell is span-rule untestable outright (14.5 years is fewer than two 11-year cycles). The audit documents what splicing conceals: on identical years, FY2023-24 growth is 9.2 per cent on the 2011-12 base against 7.2 per cent on the 2022-23 base, and FY2024-25 is 6.5 against 7.1 per cent.

*Verdicts are design-dependent on identical records.* The political budget cycle inverts the pilot's spectral verdict on state finances at annual frequency: as Khemani's panel regression on the same budgets (28 states × 34 years = 952 state-years, $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ 359), the cell is testable — MDES 0.20 pp of GSDP on capital outlay against a 0.25 pp anchor, and 0.29 pp against the 0.50 pp deficit anchor — so even that literature's well-known deficit null is a gradeable statement on the modern panel. Convergence shows the same sensitivity within one cell: beta is testable (MDES 1.21 %/yr against 1.5 %/yr), while the sigma sub-grade is underpowered, its detectable drift of 0.0030/yr sitting at the very top of the literature's 0.001–0.003/yr range. Reporting only one form reports the more or less favourable half of the same ruler.

### 3.3 The cells that should change practice

*Okun's law is untestable, and its formula number is a trap.* The naive formula on seven annual PLFS points returns MDES 0.16, apparently comfortable against the 0.30 anchor. The build explains why it must not be used: it is mechanically flattered by one leverage point, the COVID year, which inflates the standard deviation of growth while unemployment barely moved — in an economy the build describes as roughly 90 per cent informal, the shock surfaced as earnings, not open unemployment. The minimum-$n$ rule exists precisely for this cell. The pre-2017 Employment-Unemployment Survey is officially "not comparable" with the PLFS, the PLFS restarted in 2017 and was redesigned in 2025: India's most-argued labour-market relationship currently has no ruler that can grade it.

*The natural rate is published routinely and is untestable on clean data.* RBI $r^*$ estimates of 1.4–1.9 per cent exist and are used, but the joint clean span is 58 quarters against a 100-quarter floor for Laubach–Williams-class estimation. The number exists; a data-only test of it does not. Uses of a published $r^*$ should be explicit that its support is spliced series plus model structure.

*The two monsoon rulers disagree about testability itself.* On the longer IITM ruler the solar and long bands are individually testable (0.46 and 0.43 sd against the 0.47 anchor); on the IMD ruler they are underpowered (0.56 and 0.55), and the registered joint grade is the worse. Both rulers are powered only for strong cycles: in the ENSO band the detectable floor is about 19 per cent of variance. This qualifies the pilot's headline as the build states it: its null rules out dominant peaks of Kane's reported strength, but a cycle explaining a tenth of variance — the anchor — would escape the pilot's test about half the time; and Kane's own maximum-entropy method has sharper resolution than the registered periodogram test graded here. Over identical years 1901–2016 the IMD June–September mean is 887.3 mm against IITM's 843.6 mm (+5.2%, $r = 0.947$, pilot), and estimated persistence is $+0.03$ (IMD) against $-0.10$ (IITM): even the sign of the monsoon's memory depends on the ruler.

The remaining audits record the same discipline. IIP's clean span (April 2012–March 2026, base 2011-12) was ended by the 2022-23 rebasing. CPI exists only from January 2011 (clean to December 2025, base 2012), with a documented revision (January 2026: 2.75% → 2.74%), a linking factor of 0.5267 to the new base, and food weight falling from 45.86% to 36.75% on base 2024. WPI's higher residual variance makes its Phillips MDES (0.39) worse than CPI's (0.29), though the classic Indian Phillips estimates were run on WPI. Bank credit's definition has been stable since November 1960 (RBI Handbook notes), its breaks being coverage events. State finances' second ruler (Comptroller and Auditor General audited accounts) has a documented-only gap here. CGWB waves (2009, 2013, 2017, 2020, 2022–2025; GEC-97 → GEC-2015 at 2017; six like-for-like waves) are gradeable on no registered test.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 What researchers should stop arguing about — in this form

The implication is not "stop studying these questions" but "stop presenting these pairings as if they could decide them". Juglar claims on clean GDP, IIP, CPI, WPI or PLFS spans should not be presented as tests: fewer than two full cycles, and no estimator changes that arithmetic. Okun coefficients on seven PLFS points should not be presented as evidence in either direction. Data-only $r^*$ claims on the post-2011 span should be labelled model-and-splice-dependent. Monsoon claims at the tenth-of-variance anchor should not be presented as settled by the registered test, which would miss such a cycle about half the time. Phillips and Kitchin arguments on the rebased indices should be reported as underpowered, MDES stated alongside the estimate, so a null reads as "this ruler could not reliably have seen the anchored effect", not "the effect is absent". In the four testable cells, ordinary argument can proceed; the Atlas grades only resolution, not answers.

### 4.2 What could move cells between classes

Verdicts are properties of spans, inputs and designs, so they can move. The build identifies the increments, reported here without additions. *Measured inputs:* persistence, noise and all regression inputs are registered assumptions, this build having pulled no new data beyond the pilot's; a follow-up pulling the real series from the databases named in the audit and re-running the registered code is, in the build's words, the obvious next increment. Three verdicts sit within about 25 per cent of a boundary and could move — Phillips–CPI (0.29 vs 0.25), beta convergence (1.21 vs 1.5 %/yr) and PBC capital outlay (0.20 vs 0.25) — and none should be quoted without that caveat. *Cross-ruler and revision measurement:* gaps are measured for one pair only (monsoon) and documented for one more (GDP); the IIP old/new-base overlap from 2022-23 and the CPI overlap-year gap exist but were not extracted, and all systematic revision magnitudes are pending the vintages ("D") build. *Time:* new bases accumulate spans slowly — GDP base 2022-23 covers only 2022-23 to 2025-26, its back series pending and expected in December 2026 at the time of the build; CPI base-2024 has values from January 2025 but year-on-year inflation comparable only from January 2026; the PLFS monthly series from April 2025 is a future ruler, not yet a span — while each rebasing destroys a maturing span, as in June 2026 for IIP and WPI. *Design:* the budget-cycle and convergence cells show that reformulating a question as a panel, cross-section or long difference on existing records can move it without new data. For groundwater, future grading will need tests for irregular waves, not the fixed-frequency machinery here — stated as the Atlas states it, with no conclusion drawn about the future build the series powers.

### 4.3 Limitations of the grading itself

We state these explicitly, following the build's "honest gaps" section. *First,* many inputs are assumptions, not measurements — registered in advance, which protects them from tuning but does not make them measured; any computed verdict could move under measured inputs, the three boundary-adjacent cells in §4.2 being the exposed ones. *Second,* the spectral MDES grades the registered test, not every test: a sharper method (Kane's maximum-entropy method) would have a lower MDES on the same spans. *Third,* cross-ruler evidence is thin by design: only the monsoon gap is measured, the GDP gap documented, revision magnitudes documented-only, pending a vintages dataset this build does not construct. *Fourth,* the grid is a choice: questions outside it, including longer archival spans, are ungraded here, not graded untestable; the §1 novelty claim has exactly this scope. *Fifth,* the thresholds are conventions (the $2\times$ boundary, four-cycle rule, 100/160-quarter thresholds, minimum-$n$ rules), fixed before computation and applied uniformly; the published grid lets readers re-grade the same MDES values under other thresholds. *Sixth,* COVID observations are retained by R6 and enter materially (the convergence difference contains COVID; the Okun formula value is a COVID artefact); those verdicts are conditional on that choice. *Finally,* this paper reports a local build faithfully rather than re-computing it independently: every claim traces to the build files as mapped in the sources file, and where the build gives a verdict without a number (dashed cells in Table 1) none is invented.
